5. CONSOLIDATION:
(NONE WERE SEEN IN THE PAST QUESTIONS!!!!)
Consolidation:
Pulmonary consolidation can be referred to as the filling of alveolar spaces with
fluid, pus, blood, or other substances, leading to increased lung tissue density.
On a chest radiograph, signs of consolidation include:
1. A homogeneous opacity obscuring blood vessels, "air bronchograms" where air-
filled bronchi appear as dark lines within the consolidation.
2. Loss of the normal lung-soft tissue interface ("silhouette sign"), and
3. No significant volume loss in the affected area, usually with poorly defined
margins, often seen in a lobar distribution due to pneumonia.
[IMG 140.1 408x303 @x90]
Key points about consolidation on chest X-ray:
Appearance: A dense, hazy area with a relatively uniform opacity.

Air bronchograms: The most characteristic sign, where air-filled bronchi appear

as dark lines within the consolidated area.
Silhouette sign: Loss of the normal border between the lung and adjacent

structures like the heart or diaphragm due to consolidation in that area.
No volume loss: Unlike lung collapse, consolidation does not usually cause a

visible decrease in lung volume.
How to identify the location of consolidation:
Upper lobe consolidation: May obscure the aortic arch on the left side.

Middle lobe consolidation: May obscure the right heart border.

=== P141
Lower lobe consolidation: May obscure the diaphragm.

Differential Diagnoses of Pulmonary Consolidation:
1. Infectious Causes
Pneumonia (Bacterial, Viral, Fungal, Aspiration)

Tuberculosis (TB) – Primary or post-primary TB with focal consolidation

Lung abscess – Consolidation with cavitation.

Pulmonary mycoses (e.g., Histoplasmosis, Blastomycosis)

2. Non-Infectious Inflammatory Causes
Organizing pneumonia (Cryptogenic or secondary)

Eosinophilic pneumonia

Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener’s disease)

Sarcoidosis (alveolar form)

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis

3. Neoplastic Causes
Bronchoalveolar carcinoma (now called lepidic-predominant

adenocarcinoma) – Mimics pneumonia.
Lymphoma – Can present as a localized lung mass or diffuse consolidation

4. Vascular Causes
Pulmonary infarction (from pulmonary embolism) – May show a wedge-shaped

opacity.
Pulmonary edema – Cardiogenic or non-cardiogenic (e.g., ARDS).

5. Other Causes
Pulmonary hemorrhage syndromes (e.g., Goodpasture’s syndrome, Vasculitis)

Drug-induced lung disease (e.g., Amiodarone toxicity)

Radiation pneumonitis – Consolidation in previously irradiated lung fields

Key Imaging Clues for Differentiation:
Air bronchograms – More common in infectious causes.

Cavitation – Seen in TB, abscesses, fungal infections.

Peripheral consolidation – Seen in eosinophilic pneumonia, cryptogenic

organizing pneumonia.
Upper lobe involvement – TB, sarcoidosis, fungal infections.

Multifocal/migratory consolidations – Organizing pneumonia, eosinophilic

pneumonia.
